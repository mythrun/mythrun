World-gen experience

What a player watches while their world is built, and the world they land in when it's done. The phase-aware /campaigns/:id route runs a two-act generation — a quiet loading screen while the early prep stages run, then a live graph of the world assembling itself out of drifting fog — and flips to the browsable reveal the moment the world's introduction page is written (right after the connections weave), ~10 minutes before the pipeline actually finishes. The reveal opens the settled graph beside a WorldWiki whose front door is that introduction. The fog is a real fluid simulation, the graph a contained force-directed layout, and both run in a DOM-free client-core brain that @mythrun/ui paints onto layered canvases.

How it fits together

The experience follows the frontend's own brain/render split (see the design system). The client-core/worldgen subpath is the platform-neutral brain — the SSE fold, the fog fluid sim, the force graph, the pannable camera, the wiki normalizer, the countdown math — none of it touches the DOM. @mythrun/ui turns that brain into pixels: a fog canvas, a graph canvas, a status card, a loading screen, and the browsable wiki. @mythrun/web's CampaignDetailRoute composes them and owns the lifecycle. The route consumes three backend surfaces — the campaign status SSE stream, the stage-timings ETA endpoint, and the owner world-snapshot — and never reaches past a client-core hook to do it.

Why a DOM-free physics core — the fog sim and the force graph are pure data-in/data-out simulations: obstacles and a clock go in, a density grid and node positions come out. Keeping them in client-core (like the design system's contract layer) means the same physics can be unit-tested with an injected RNG, driven by a Storybook tuning harness with synthetic entities, and — when the native app is written — reused behind a native canvas without a rewrite. The web layer owns only the platform-bound edges: the requestAnimationFrame loop, device-pixel-ratio sizing, pointer events, and the prefers-reduced-motion read.

The phase-aware route

CampaignDetailRoute is the home-hub card's click target and where the create flow lands after a world starts generating. It opens the status stream through useWorldGenStream, which folds the SSE frames into three things the whole experience reads: the graph's nodes and edges, the per-stage stages progress, and a coarse lifecycle status/phase. The route branches on that lifecycle: a generating world shows the two-act generation; a failed one shows a calm retryable resume screen; a terminal one a dead-end nudge; a denied one a calm no-access screen; and a finished world shows either the reveal or — once the owner has picked a starting situation — the campaign page.

A denied campaign is a terminal dead end, not a retry (myth-otukr) — when the status stream's open returns 403/404 — not a member, no such campaign, a malformed id — no phase frame ever arrives, so the route would otherwise strand on the phase === null settling line forever. The stream client settles failed{denied} and stops retrying; useWorldGenStream folds that into a coarse denied status that wins over every phase-derived status (the connection failed before any frame could land), and the route checks it before the settling gate and renders NoAccessView — a calm no-access screen with no retry affordance, because retrying an access denial can't grant access. This is deliberately narrower than the transient exhausted connection state (the backoff ceiling on a network outage), which the stream leaves re-armable rather than folding to a dead end: a denial is a route-level dead end, a passing outage is not.
Why world_ready needs a second read — picking a starting situation doesn't advance the pipeline phase (it only records an index on the campaign), so a just-generated world and a world whose owner already picked look identical at the phase level: both are complete/world_ready. The route disambiguates by fetching the chosen-situation flag for exactly that one case; every later phase implies a situation is already chosen and routes straight to the campaign page. A brief settling line covers the flag's resolve window so the reveal-vs-page split never flashes the wrong surface.

Act one — the loading screen

Until the first entity-bearing stage runs there is nothing to draw — the early stages (concept_intake, world_color, vocabulary_intake) produce registry and tone context, never a graph node. So act one is the WorldGenLoading screen, and it is a calm screen rather than a busy one: the player has nothing to do here, so the surface holds a beat instead of simulating activity. It has four parts. An accreting dither mark — a 1-bit square whose quantized fill is driven by real pipeline events (completed stages and arrived entities), so structural resolution means work happened and stillness means stillness. Directly under it, the wait line: a coarse "ready in about N minutes," or the stalled stand-in when the estimate runs out. Below those, one whisper of flavor copy at headline size, with the whispers already shown settling beneath it as a faint ledger. And a closing line giving permission to leave, paired with the promise that makes it credible — re-entering lands back on this wait with progress intact, which is real product behavior, not reassurance.

The mark is driven by events, not by a clock — a time-driven animation cannot tell "working" from "hung": it keeps swirling either way, so a player who returns to a wedged pipeline sees exactly what a healthy one looks like. Filling the mark from completed stages and arrived entities makes it an honest ambient progress signal — the returning player's proof of life is that the mark has more ink than when they left. At zero accretion it runs a faint idle shimmer, poised at the fill's origin, deliberately fainter than the first real accretion so the handoff never reads as a reset: ink only ever accrues.
The ledger is the progress texture — a flavor line that merely swaps itself out leaves no evidence that the wait has been lived through. Letting each retired whisper settle into a short, age-faded ledger below the current one turns the cycle into a visible record: the column grows as the minutes pass. The order is a shuffled permutation walked to exhaustion and reshuffled with no repeat across the boundary, so a ten-minute wait never replays the same line twice in a row. The ledger is decorative prose and is hidden from assistive tech entirely; the surface's aria-busy and its live region carry the real state. Under prefers-reduced-motion the cycle never advances, so the ledger simply stays empty.
Every slot is reserved from first paint — the estimate arrives late, the watch affordance arrives later, and a stall notice may never arrive at all. Each of them appearing into an unreserved slot shoved the composition down the screen mid-wait. So the layout reserves the height for the wait line, the watch beat, and the hero whisper's second line up front: an element being absent and then present is fine, but the rest of the screen moving because of it is not, on a surface the player stares at for minutes.

The instant entities begin arriving — a first node, or an entity stage reported underway, whichever lands first — the loading screen grows a "watch your world be born" affordance in its reserved slot. Generation keeps streaming underneath regardless; the button only reveals the graph. The transition to act two is a deliberate press, never automatic — but only on a genuine first visit. A refresh or reconnect that lands mid-generation with committed entities already present (resumedMidGen: a non-empty connect-time seed on the stream's first frame) skips straight to the graph act with its nodes already rehydrated, because the deliberate loading beat is a one-time thing to mark, not a wall to re-cross on every reload. The transition is also two-way: a quiet link on the act-two status card returns the player to the calm wait, so watching the world assemble is something they can put down.

The split between "prep" and "entity" stages is derived once from the canonical WORLD_GEN_STAGES taxonomy (PREP_STAGES vs ENTITY_STAGES in @mythrun/contract/events), so a taxonomy change can never drift the boundary.

The graph survives a refresh — the folded nodes and edges live only in the client's in-memory SSE accumulation, so a reload during generation would otherwise return the player to an empty loading screen and lose everything committed so far. The stream's connect-time snapshot now carries a known-tier graph seed — the same entities and edges the live stream had already delivered to this member — and useWorldGenStream folds it exactly like a live frame, so a mid-gen reconnect rehydrates a populated graph and keeps folding toward the reveal. The seed is known-tier by the same invariant the live stream is, so a reconnecting client is caught up with zero new exposure. That snapshot→nodes/edges projection was extracted into a canonical adapter, snapshotToGraph, in MYT-94 — the same one the in-session Relationship Graph tab renders its known-tier edges from — so both consumers share one projection rather than a divergent second.

Act two — the fog and the graph

Act two is the world assembling itself. Two canvases are layered: a ground of land and weather behind, and the entity graph in front. As each entity streams in its node appears as its complete square ink glyph, and as relationships arrive their edges stroke in as straight penstrokes rather than snapping. The nodes lay out under a force-directed simulation that clusters the graph as edges connect it, and the whole thing is draggable. The status card sits over the canvas (a fixed-count progress bar naming its own scope — "stage 2 of 4" — plus the wait line and the return-to-the-wait link); on a phone it drops out of the canvas into a compact footer.

An arriving node is fully drawn on its first frame — each node used to arrive with a short "trace-then-stamp" beat: a penstroke outlining the silhouette before the solid ink landed. At real arrival rates that read as flicker rather than ceremony, and it made an incomplete-looking glyph a normal sight on a screen whose whole job is to convince the player the world is real. Edges keep their penstroke draw-in — a connection genuinely is drawn between two places — but a place simply exists.

The ground is land, not only weather

Behind the graph sits a static landmass field: a seeded value-noise height map with a sea level, painted in the same square cells, the same dither, and the same ink as the fog. Inside a landmass the cells are dense; outside it the paper is left alone, so the emptiness reads as sea rather than as an unfinished render. The fog still rolls over the top of it and drains away as entities arrive. Land is the world; fog is the weather that lifts off it.

One structure answered two separate complaints — a wind-advected particle field has no coastline and no silhouette, so the map read as a diagonal spray of confetti signalling "pixels appearing" rather than "a place"; and because the sim spawns from the wind edges, the lower stretch of the canvas sat empty in every frame. Separately, the field's contrast was failing for a reason a stylesheet check couldn't see: a solid painted cell measured well above the non-text floor, but only about a quarter of the pane's pixels were painted, so the field the eye integrates measured far lower. No ink change rescues a low-coverage dither — the fix has to be coverage, which means the field needs somewhere it is genuinely dense. Land is that somewhere. Its coverage floor is what carries the integrated contrast past the floor, and the guarantee is pinned by a test measuring actually-rendered pixels rather than declared colours.
The terrain paint is a semantic token, and it diverges from the reference on purpose — the reference prototype's fog quads were tuned against a dev backdrop; over the shipped page they blended to nearly nothing, which made the thing the player stares at for eight minutes the faintest element on the screen. The fog's own parameter record stays a verbatim copy of that prototype, but the production paint takes its ink from the theme-aware --worldgen-terrain alias at full alpha, so the painted cell is the token's own ink in both themes and the near-black node glyphs and labels still out-contrast it comfortably.

The world stays where the player saw it

A force layout keeps annealing as arrivals and edges reheat it, which means a place the player has already seen named can drift somewhere else minutes later. Two mechanisms hold it still. The simulation is only re-synced when the graph's content actually changed — a fingerprint over the folded nodes and edges — so a re-render caused by something else entirely (expanding the wiki pane, selecting an entity) no longer injects reheat energy into a settled constellation. And once the field has genuinely cooled, every labelled node is pinned where it sits. New entities still settle freely around the pinned constellation: the world may grow; what the player has watched take its place does not move.

Positional stability outranks a tidier layout — pinning costs something real: a pinned node no longer migrates toward its community when later edges arrive, so the finished layout crosses more lines than a fully-free one would. That trade is deliberate. For a feature sold as "watch your world take shape," a place that wanders is poison — the fiction is a fixed world being revealed, and a layout that keeps improving itself contradicts it. Pins are taken only below a settle threshold, never mid-flight (a node pinned at its seed position would freeze the layout unformed), and a node stays pinned even if its label later retires under the density budget: the label tier breathes, the place must not. Dragging overrides the pin while held, and on the reveal a released node flies home and re-pins where it lands.

Re-entry is the other half. The live map is an unmountable surface — returning to the calm wait unmounts it, and so does leaving the route — so the settled layout is kept in a session-scoped layout cache keyed by campaign: the pinned placements (keyed by each node's stable identity, so a later finalize swapping its display id doesn't lose the placement) and the terrain seed. On re-entry the cached nodes pin straight back where they were and the same coastline is drawn, so the second visit reads as the first plus accretion.

Layout is world state, not render state — while the layout lived only in component refs, every re-entry re-seeded both the force simulation and the terrain's random field, so every named place jumped hundreds of pixels and the continent re-rolled wholesale. "The world keeps forming" became "the world keeps being regenerated," which is the opposite of the thing being sold. The cache is a module map with no storage behind it, deliberately: a full page refresh restarts the stream and re-snapshots the world anyway, so the fiction worth protecting is within-session re-entry, and memory is bounded by campaigns visited. The seed needs no such caveat — it is derived from the campaign key, so the coastline survives a refresh for free.

The graph also ships a legend. Entity kind is encoded in the shape of a node's ink glyph, and a symbol system nobody documents is decoration rather than information — un-named glyphs read as a rendering glitch. The key is derived, not fixed: it renders one entry per kind actually present in the world, in a stable vocabulary order, each only when the composing surface supplied a resolved label. It also names the hollow glyph — an outline-only node whose name has retired under the density budget — because a screenful of unexplained outlines reads as breakage too.

The fog is a fluid simulation, not a texture — FogSim advects tracer particles through a velocity field of wind, turbulence, and potential-flow doublets around each entity, bins them into a density grid, and renders that grid as a crisp ordered-Bayer dither in the brand's 1-bit register — no alpha gradients. Every interlocking constant was dialed in by eye in a reference prototype and ported verbatim; "close enough" was explicitly not the bar. The sim runs on a fixed timestep so it looks identical at 60Hz and 120Hz, and its particle count scales with canvas area so the tuned density holds at every pane size.
The fog clears by entity count, not by a fade — the atmosphere thins as the world fills, but it does it by starving the spawn: every arrived node ramps the particle-respawn flux down by one over MIN_EXPECTED_ENTITIES (the minimum entity count a real generation produces, derived from real worlds), so the wind carries the existing fog off the pane and ever fewer particles replace it. By the time the graph is full the fog is genuinely gone — a physical clearing, not a global opacity dialed toward zero. The earlier procedural GhostBloomField ambient layer was removed; the fog replaces it entirely.
One virtual coordinate space, CSS-scaled — the fog constants are absolute pixels tuned at a fixed reference size, so the sim and the graph both render into a shared virtual coordinate space that is then CSS-scaled to the actual pane. That single derivation is what keeps the fog's obstacles aligned with the graph's nodes at every width and aspect, and it is where the "a node can never escape the viewport" containment closes in real pixels — the force sim clamps each node's box (icon plus label) inside the virtual frame, and the shared scale carries that guarantee to the screen.

On a phone the graph does not shrink the world to fit — it becomes a camera over it. The full, legible world is laid out at its natural size and the pane pans across it with one finger (the fog panning in lockstep), gently following new arrivals until the player takes over. The FullBleedStage primitive cancels the app frame's content inset so the pannable canvas fills the screen edge-to-edge below the header, with the status footer as the only chrome.

A camera, not a shrink or a forced rotation — at real entity counts a whole world squeezed into a 375px pane is unreadable, and forcing landscape is unenforceable in a mobile browser (the orientation-lock API needs fullscreen and has no iOS support). Panning a full-scale world keeps every label legible and tappable and degrades gracefully to portrait. The camera is render-only: it moves what's drawn, never the true node positions the fog's clear-zones and the hit targets key on.

The reveal — the graph beside the introduction

The visible wait ends the moment the world's introduction page is written. When world_introduction completes (connectionsDone over the folded stages — the predicate keeps its name, but its boundary stage moved here from entity_relationships), the route swaps the generating view for WorldReveal — even though the pipeline's tail stages (entity_consolidation through embed_world) are still running. The reveal fetches the world-snapshot (readable by any campaign member since myth-0xwos, so a party member's reveal renders too, not just the owner's) and lays the settled graph beside a browsable WorldWiki whose front door is the introduction: the world's name as the title, a few paragraphs of grounding prose, and an ordered list of dig-deeper references, with the search field and the browse-by-kind index below it. There is no separate entity list — the wiki is the browse surface.

A world that carries a glossary of invented vocabulary gets a second peer view rather than a longer scroll. A segmented switch splits the panel into index (the introduction, then search and the browse-by-kind groups) and glossary (the vocabulary as a scannable list, mono term over serif definition). The switch sits above the scroll region so it never scrolls away; only the active view scrolls beneath it. A world with no glossary keeps the single-view landing with no switch at all — the graceful absence, not an empty tab.

An entity page is a drill-in, not a third segment — opening an entity swaps the whole landing for that page and back, and the switch's active view is owned by the shell rather than the landing, so the round-trip returns the reader to the view they left. Making the entity page a third segment would have implied it sits beside index and glossary as a peer, which it doesn't: there is one entity page at a time and it is reached from a view.
The reveal waits for the introduction, not the raw graph — the boundary used to be entity_relationships: the reveal fired the instant the connections were woven and the player landed on a bare graph. A world_introduction stage now runs inside the boundary — right after the connections weave, before the silent tail — so the wait ends on a page of prose that tells the player what world they are in. By the time they have watched the graph assemble for the better part of ten minutes, the last thing they need is another graph; they need the elevator pitch. Consolidation (which only adds phantom-resolution ghosts, never removes the entities the intro references) stays in the tail, so nothing the introduction points at can vanish behind the reveal.

The layout has two regimes, flipped structurally on the design system's one narrow breakpoint (useViewportNarrow). On desktop the reveal is a true 50/50: RevealPanes holds the interactive graph in the left half and the wiki in the right — two equal min-width-0 halves — with the graph filling its pane in both dimensions (it never pans there) and the wiki capping to the row and scrolling internally. On a phone the graph owns the glass, on the same FullBleedStage full-bleed mechanism as the generating act, and the wiki lives in an edge-handle Drawer: a sheet summoned from a handle pinned to the right edge, also opened by tapping a node. The drawer's children stay mounted across close→open, so the reader's page and scroll survive a dismiss; the reveal's title, explore prompt, and Continue ride the sheet, keeping the base screen pure world.

Each entity opens to its own wiki page. Since MYT-102 the page leads with the entity's own prose — its prose kernel, the paragraph that says what the thing is — above its known facts, then a RELATED footer of relationship cross-links, with a back button that restores the previous page and its scroll position. Before that, an entry could surface only an oblique fact ("wake-crows work the Ironwake without a Salvage Charter") that never introduced the thing it described; the kernel body is the fix. Selecting a node highlights its wiki page and vice-versa: one shared selection, because after generation every node's identity is its real entity id, the same id the wiki keys on, so no translation is needed.

The wiki also has to announce that it is a scroll region. It is a fixed-height pane — the graph half owns the row's height and the wiki half caps to it — but macOS overlay scrollbars vanish at rest and nothing marked the cut edge, so a definition running past the fold read as a hard clip mid-sentence rather than as "there is more." WikiScrollFrame supplies both halves of the affordance: a quiet scrollbar styled into the ink register with a stable reserved gutter (so expanding a glossary row never reflows the prose sideways), and top and bottom edge fades gated by scroll position. Rendered prose also passes through a mechanical smart-quote substitution — the generator writes typewriter quotes, which inside a bookish serif definition read as a typo.

The edge fade is a mask on the content, not a wash over it — the first cut painted a page-coloured gradient over the content, which failed twice. On a panel grounded on a different surface tone the wash was visibly the wrong colour, a grey smudge; and it rendered whole glossary rows at intermediate alpha, which is the near-universal styling for disabled — exactly the wrong thing to imply about a tappable term. Fading the content itself to transparent with a CSS mask reveals whatever ground is genuinely behind, on any panel tone and in either theme, by construction.
The prose was always on the wire; the wiki just wasn't showing it — the known-tier world-snapshot already carried each entity's prose kernel and the world's vocabulary registry. MYT-102 added the display-only projection (proseKernels per entity, a term/definition glossary) to the frontend snapshot contract and rendered it — the kernel as the entity body, the glossary as the home's closing section. It is a display change on the client, not new content and not a new tier boundary: the server's known-tier filter still decides which kernels and glossary entries ship, so nothing secret reaches the projection.
Fetching the snapshot mid-tail is enforced-ignorance-safe — the reveal fetches the world-snapshot at the same world_introduction boundary the endpoint's readiness gate keys on, so it never races the 409. The known-tier filter is entirely server-side: the snapshot returns only known entities, facts, relationships, and the introduction (its dig-deeper references re-filtered against the served entity set), so fetching it while the tail is still running serves exactly the same player-safe projection as fetching it after completion. The client renders what it's given; it holds no secret it must remember to hide.

While the player explores, the tail runs silently. Each tail completion quietly refetches the wiki snapshot — the reconciled world just appears, no spinner takeover, the old model held on screen until the fresh one resolves — and the graph absorbs the same patches through the still-open stream. The "Continue" affordance stays hidden until the pipeline actually finishes, because the starting-situation step it leads to needs a complete world; it appears quietly when the tail lands.

The reveal moment

The swap from the generating graph to the reveal is choreographed, and it plays exactly once — for the player who watched their world build. RevealTransition wraps the reveal and walks a short timeline: a hold breath on the cleared paper, a gust beat, the world settles as the title fades in, and the layout resolves — on desktop the full-width graph re-seats to the left half while the wiki pane slides in from the right; on mobile the drawer's edge handle arrives. Then it is done: the steady state, identical to a reveal that never played. The walk latches on its first frame, so the later world-ready flip neither restarts nor reverses it, and any tap or keypress mid-moment lands the player in the final state at once (the interaction still does what it would have done).

The moment is for the watcher, never the late arriver — a player returning to an already-settled world mounts the reveal in its final state with no choreography. The beat marks the turn from watching to exploring; there is nothing to mark for someone who didn't watch. Under prefers-reduced-motion the whole walk collapses to a single ~300ms surface fade — the completion is still acknowledged, just not animated.
The settled world insists on its shape — the reveal runs the graph under a "forceful settle" force profile distinct from the generating act's: the layout pulls itself into a legible arrangement, and a node the player drags and releases snaps back rather than staying where it was dropped — the world erases your mess. The regime is additive: reveal-only parameters layered over the same locked simulation, so the generating act's behavior is untouched.
The fog is born gone — on the reveal the clearing is not re-run; the sim mounts with its spawn fully drained, so the world arrives with no atmosphere left whatever its size. A small world's residual haze was tried as a final live gust and cut: a freshly-mounted sim's cold low-pass fades the haze back in before the wind can carry it off, an inverted arc. The invariant is simply "the fog is gone," enforced from the swap.

Waiting feedback

Both acts carry a wait estimate so the player always holds an expectation of the wait, and since the generating view stays mounted across the act-one → act-two swap, there is exactly one budget behind both: the remaining time to the wiki reveal. The route fetches the stage-timings ETA endpoint once at the start; a slice-summing core sums the historical per-stage averages over the canonical watched-stage slice, intersected with the live stage statuses and minus the time already spent in the running stage, and a one-second interval re-derives it. The slice derives from the same canonical WORLD_GEN_STAGES taxonomy the pipeline does, so a stage reorder can't drift the target.

What reaches the screen is three folds away from that raw number, and each fold answers a way the raw number lies. It is clamped monotonic — the shown wait may hold, never climb. It is damped — the first estimate is adopted whole, then the displayed value steps down at most one whole minute per whisper dwell. And it is rendered coarse: a minute-granular phrase, never a ticking 0:24. When the estimate exhausts — a final stage overrunning, or a wedged stage keeping the number positive well past a generous multiple of the first estimate — the line degrades in language to a stalled reassurance rather than sitting frozen on a number. With no timing history at all (a cold start) there is no line: no history is not a fake instant estimate.

Direction and cadence are separate problems, and both had to be fixed — the monotonic clamp handles a number that would otherwise climb, which reads as the system losing ground. It does nothing about a number that falls too eagerly: an early build shed two minutes in forty-five seconds, because every re-estimate surfaced the instant it was computed, and the result read as nervous guessing rather than an estimate. The damper fixes the cadence by pacing the display to the whisper dwell; exhaustion deliberately bypasses it, because the stalled line is honesty rather than display. The same reasoning kills per-second precision: a ticking countdown on a ten-minute LLM pipeline is false precision plus per-second churn on a screen meant to be calm.
The status card names its own scope — its bars count only the watched entity stages, so the bar reaches 100% exactly at the reveal moment; a card that kept counting the silent tail would strand at ~60% and then vanish when the reveal fired. But a bare stage label beside a small percentage invited reading that percentage as overall progress. Each bar now carries its position explicitly ("stage 2 of 4"), so stage scope and overall time can't be conflated — the overall answer is the wait line's job. The player never sees API cost on this card either; spend is a dev-tool concern, not a player-facing number.
One wait vocabulary across every long wait — the estimate → stalled → hidden decision, the coarse bucketing, and the damper live in a shared client-core/loading module rather than in this surface. Character creation and the between-sessions session-prep hold read the same brain with different inputs, so the three multi-minute waits in the product speak with one voice and a fix to how a wait degrades lands everywhere at once.

The parked hold

One state overrides the whole wait vocabulary: a park. When the world-gen job contains a connectivity or rate-limit failure and parks itself to auto-resume, useWorldGenStream surfaces lastErrorClass (+ resumeAttempt), and while it is set the hold copy — worldgen.degradedHold, "The making was interrupted. Your world is safe. It keeps trying on its own." — owns the wait narrative on both acts. The countdown suppresses, and the shared.wait.stalled line never shows: "Still working" and "the making was interrupted" must never share a screen. When the park clears, the ETA damper and monotonic-clamp refs reset and re-adopt a fresh estimate — a resumed generation may honestly need more time than the pre-park floor, and pinning the stale minimum would show the stalled line for the healthy remainder.

The park state arrives two ways, deliberately asymmetric. The authoritative delivery is the (re)connect snapshot — derivePhase surfaces the persisted stamp's class and rung, and the hook sets its park state from every snapshot frame, including back to null when the payload omits the stamp — because CloudFront buffers SSE and the generating view invites leave-and-return, so the class must re-derive on every reconnect. The live events are only an in-window optimization: a park-eligible stage-failed.failureClass arms the hold immediately, and any stage-completed clears it (a completed stage ends the park episode). The reveal surface is a touchpoint too — six of twelve stages run behind the reveal boundary, so a tail-stage park swaps the quiet worldReveal.settling line for the stamp-keyed worldReveal.degradedSettling variant rather than leaving an indefinite unexplained "the table is still being set". The sibling hold keys for the other two prep waits — characterCreate.degradedHold, sessionPrep.degradedHold — landed with the same copy discipline, but their consuming surfaces are not wired yet; today the world-gen views are the only park-aware waits.

Reduced motion

Every motion source honours prefers-reduced-motion, read once in the web layer and handed to the brain as a boolean: the fog's settle, the node bob, the edge draw-ins, the loading screen's whisper cycle, and the reveal-moment choreography. Under the preference the fog recomputes a static settled density on each entity-count change (preserving the clearing-as-progress cue rather than freezing blank), the bob stops, edges appear finished instead of stroking in, and the whisper holds on one line; the reveal moment collapses to a single ~300ms fade. What does not stop is progress: the countdown keeps ticking and the screen-reader live region keeps announcing. Those are content, not decoration — freezing the countdown would show a wrong number, and freezing the announcer would strand a non-sighted reader mid-generation.

The accessible model

Both canvases — the fog and the graph — are aria-hidden decoration: a screen reader cannot perceive a pixel of them, and a keyboard cannot land on a node drawn to a canvas. Left at that, the whole ten-minute generation act and the reveal's graph would be silent and unreachable. So each act pairs its canvas with a VisuallyHidden model that carries the same information into the accessibility tree — nothing paints, but everything is announced and navigable.

During generation, GenerationAnnouncer renders two things: an aria-live="polite" region announcing the running phase and a rough entity tally, and a hidden list of the entities that have taken shape so far — a snapshot the reader can step through at will. The visible entity list was cut from the design; this SR-only list is its accessibility-tree counterpart, not a mirror of anything on screen.

The live region coalesces, so a burst announces once — entities stream in flurries, and a live region bound naively to the count would machine-gun the reader with twenty announcements in a second. So a phase change flushes the region immediately (a stage transition is news to hear at once), while an entity-tally change is trailing-debounced over a ~1.5s quiet window — a burst collapses to a single "…24 entities…". The determinate percentage stays the status card's progressbar; this region carries only what the canvas hides — that entities are appearing, the phase, and roughly how many.

In the reveal, the guaranteed keyboard-and-screen-reader path to every entity is the wiki's browse-by-kind index — a real list of links, no canvas involved. Beside it, GraphNodeList makes the graph itself reachable: a hidden role="listbox" with roving tabindex, where arrow keys walk the nodes and Enter selects one through the same onSelect a canvas tap fires — so a keyboard user drives the identical graph-highlight-plus-wiki-push a mouse user does. Selection mirrors both ways: a tap or a wiki pick moves the roving position onto that node, so the keyboard entry point always tracks whatever the world last selected.

On a phone the wiki lives in the edge-handle Drawer, and Back has to mean the right thing there. DrawerBackClose gives the open drawer Back precedence: while it is open, a browser or hardware Back closes the drawer and stays on the reveal route; a second Back leaves as normal — the sheet-dismisses-first behavior a phone user expects. The drawer's own focus contract carries the rest: opening moves focus into the sheet, and closing returns it to the edge handle, so the keyboard never strands on an invisible control.

Back precedence rides the router's history, not window.history — DrawerBackClose intercepts the POP through react-router's useBlocker; a manual pushState would corrupt the data router's history bookkeeping. Only a POP while the drawer is open is blocked, so a PUSH (Continue → the starting-situation route) flows through untouched. The wiki's own in-surface Back button still pops the wiki page stack; wiring browser Back to that as well would mean lifting WorldWiki's encapsulated navigation out of the component, so page-back stays the wiki's affordance while browser Back manages the drawer→route layers.

Operating this piece

Watch a world generate locally

  1. Bring up the stack: pnpm db:up, pnpm dev (backend + worker), and the web app (pnpm --filter @mythrun/web dev). A real generation needs live Anthropic credentials configured in backend/.env.development.
  2. Sign in and create a campaign from the hub. The app routes to /campaigns/:id and the loading screen appears while the prep stages run.
  3. When the "watch your world be born" affordance appears, press it to enter act two and watch the fog clear off the land as entities and edges stream in. The status card's quiet link returns you to the calm wait, and going back to the map should replay the same coastline with every named place where you left it — a re-rolled continent or a migrated place is the layout cache failing. The world reveals the moment world_introduction completes (right after the connections weave); the wiki quietly reconciles as the tail finishes.
  4. To inspect the underlying events, open the same campaign in the dev tool's wire-log drawer — it streams the identical SSE frames the graph is folding.

Re-tune the graph spread

  1. Open the WorldGenGraph Storybook, which exposes a live tuning harness with sliders over the force parameters (repulsion, link strength, collision padding, margin) and a max-density fixture.
  2. Dial the sliders against the dense fixture until the layout reads well, then copy the values back into the locked FORCE_PARAMS defaults in client-core/worldgen. The story is a dev-only surface; the committed defaults are the canonical spec.

Integration points

The route reads the backend world-gen pipeline through the campaign status SSE stream, the stage-timings ETA endpoint, and the owner world-snapshot — whose known-tier filtering (including the relationship tiering that keeps a secret connection between two public entities off the reveal) is the enforced-ignorance boundary this experience relies on. The campaign hub is where a card routes in; the settled world hands off to the campaign page once a starting situation is chosen. Every rendered piece is a mythrunUI component, and the SSE fold rides the same client-core transport primitives as the rest of the app. Cover and node identity trace back to the world colour identity.
